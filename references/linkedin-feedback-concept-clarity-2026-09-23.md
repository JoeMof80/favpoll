# Why the poll doesn't land — a reader's reaction

**23rd September 2026.** Diagnosis of one piece of unprompted feedback, from a
friend sent the favpoll link on LinkedIn.

---

## The feedback

> "Fab Poll looks very professional you have done a fantastic job, I am not sure
> I get the idea through, I get it is for raising sponsorship money for charity
> but what is the vote poll thing about?"

---

## He didn't fail to follow the steps — he followed them and asked what they're for

Read the home page end to end and every line is an instruction:

- **Hero** — pick, give, see
- **Process** — scan, view, pick, pledge, reveal, watch, print

The page answers *how* in nine places and *why* in none. "What is the vote poll
thing about?" is a why question, and there is nothing on the page aimed at it.

## The pick and the pledge read as two things, because they are two sentences

The idea is that they are one act — you give money, and what you give *with* it
is a piece of yourself; what comes back is a standing and, sometimes, the
person's own favourite.

But "Pick your favourite. Give what it's worth." is a list, and a list of two
things reads as two things: a donation, and then a poll bolted on. That is
exactly what he reported back.

## The referent left home with the demo

The brand doc records this itself: the headline before the current one "had been
written to caption" the hero demo, and the demo moved to the register pages in
#519–#522.

The register cards now carry the honour — so a visitor who doesn't click one
never meets a person whose favourite matters. Without Belinda and purple, "pick
your favourite" *is* a survey. He read the page exactly as written.

## And the name is doing half the damage

He wrote "Fab Poll" — twice. He decoded **poll** (survey, votes) instantly and
never decoded **fav** as *favourite*.

So he arrived already expecting a voting tool, which is the one thing the brand
doc insists favpoll is not, and then went looking for why the voting was there.
The word that imports the wrong model is in the product name, which means the
page has to do more work than a neutral name would, not less.

## What he saw first also matters

The LinkedIn preview is the title `favpoll` plus the brand statement as the
description:

> Expressions of joy, for charitable causes, in the name of those we love.

That is soul, not explanation. It sets no frame for a poll at all, so he landed
already reaching for a familiar category, found charity money, and slotted it
next to JustGiving.

---

## Caveat

This is one reader, primed by a friend's link, possibly on a phone. But his
confusion lands precisely on the structural gap the page already has — which
makes it more credible, not less.

---

## Where to go, in order of value for effort

### 1. Put a referent back above the fold

Not new doctrine — restoring what the demo used to supply. One concrete example
where a named person's favourite is the point makes "pick your favourite" mean
something.

This is the root cause, and it is the one change that would have answered his
question before he asked it.

### 2. Say what the poll is for, once

A single line that names the exchange rather than the steps. This touches
settled hero copy (2026-08-06), so it is a founder call and founder wording.

### 3. Reframe the link preview

Cheapest of the three, and it is the first thing anyone on LinkedIn sees.

---

**Recommendation: start with 1.** Options 2 and 3 are patches over a missing
referent, and the question may stop being asked once it is back.

# The payoff taxonomy

If the poll is "just an arbitrary way to donate to charity", it has no point.
A payoff is whatever makes the pick non-arbitrary. There are more of them than
notes and enactments — and three are already built, described as features.

## The pattern underneath

The payoff follows from what the pick is *about*:

| The pick is about… | Payoff | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Them** | reciprocity — they share back | the personal note |
| **The world** | consequence — it happens | therapy dog named, marathon hat worn |
| **You** | belonging, permanence, stakes | the record, the guest book, the standings |

The first two are the ones already in play. They are also the two
**non-universal** ones: notes are optional and protagonist-shaped, enactment is
fundraiser-shaped and rides custom topics. Everything in the third column
happens on *every* favpoll.

## Built already — framed as mechanics, not payoffs

**Stakes.** Your pick is in a race. "Purple climbs because Belinda loved
purple." The oldest payoff there is, and the reason anyone cares about a poll at
all. The site says "See where it stands" — an instruction. It is a payoff
wearing an instruction's clothes.

**Permanence.** Your favourite joins an all-time ranking and outlives the
occasion. The copy is already payoff-shaped — "Every pledge makes an eternal
mark" — and then says **"Coming soon"**, which cancels it.

**Presence.** The guest book: your name beside everyone else's, and what each of
them backed. You find out who else came and whether anyone picked with you.
Mild alone, but real and live.

## Not built — worth considering

**Recognition.** "You and 13 others picked Exmoor" / "you were the only one." A
read on yourself against the group. Trivial from data already held, and the
cheapest emotional payoff available.

**The mirror.** For a *living* protagonist the payoff runs the other way: Dave
finds out what his friends would bring him. A birthday favpoll's real gift may
be the aggregate going *to* the person, not a note coming *from* them. The
living-protagonist case currently borrows the memorial's shape.

**Enactment by aggregate.** The top three songs become the playlist; the top
flowers go in the arrangement. Softer than a single winner, which matters at a
wake where "winning" reads crass. Extends enactment into memorial territory
where a single-winner enactment cannot go.

**The group portrait.** Not a winner at all, but *this is what this room loves*.
Arguably what the keepsake is already for, unnamed.

## One to be careful with

**Guessing.** Let guests guess the protagonist's favourite before the note
discloses it. Powerful — and the shortest path back to Hannah's gambling
misread (stake money, guess, find out if you were right). Leave it alone.

# Topic fit — a separate axis (founder, 2026-09-23)

The payoffs above all answer *what do I get back?* Theme fit answers a different
question: *why this topic, here?*

> "A dinosaur theme with a 'favourite dinosaur' poll is self-explanatory. It's a
> way for every guest to engage with the theme."

Nobody asks why dinosaurs at a dinosaur party. The occasion has already made the
topic salient, every guest already has a relationship to it (they came, they
dressed up), and the poll becomes a way of *participating in the theme* rather
than answering a survey.

This matters because **arbitrariness is a fit problem before it is a payoff
problem.** "Favourite colour" at a memorial is arbitrary unless Belinda loved
colours — the note is what rescues it. "Favourite dinosaur" at a dinosaur party
needs no rescuing: it needs no note and no enactment, because the topic is
already motivated.

Two levers, then, not one:

1. **Payoff** — what the guest gets back for picking
2. **Fit** — why this topic belongs at this occasion

A favpoll with strong fit can carry a weak payoff. A favpoll with a weak fit
needs a strong payoff to survive. Gary's question — "what is the vote poll thing
about?" — is asked of a page that demonstrates neither.

## Theme fit feeds the record — enactment undermines it

Checked against the catalogue: **Dinosaur is already a topic.** So are Mythical
creature, Sea creature, Fairy tale, Decade, Music era, Football team, Cake,
Sweet, Board game, Toy, James Bond, Christmas song, Carol, Christmas tradition,
Cocktail and Musical. A whole theme-shaped family, already curated.

This is the structural argument for theme fit over enactment:

| | Topic | Effect on the record | Repeats? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Enacted poll** | custom (name the dog) | undermines it | one-off by nature |
| **Themed party** | catalogue (`Dinosaur`) | **feeds** it | constantly, everywhere |

The 2026-09-17 refinement parked enacted polls as "never a platform lean — it
rides custom topics, which undermine the record". Theme fit carries no such
cost. A dinosaur party runs on catalogue `Dinosaur`, and every guest's pick
piles into the all-time Dinosaur ranking.

And themed parties **repeat**. You name a therapy dog once; dinosaur parties
happen constantly. Theme fit is the use case that accumulates — which is exactly
what the record needs in order to become real. Christmas alone (a party,
`Favourite Christmas song`, a charity) is seasonal, recurring and enormous.

If there is a lean that does not cost the data project, this is it.

## The demo may be manufacturing the problem (founder, 2026-09-23)

> "For Yvette and Gemma, I just showed them the Joan & Arthur Favourite Seaside
> Town for Alzheimer's Society. Because they are fictional and the topic is
> seemingly arbitrary, it may seem pointless at first. Maybe it doesn't seem
> pointless to me because I get the idea already."

This reframes the Yvette finding. Her recorded stall — the first example "felt
pointless" until she saw the note — was measured on a **fictional protagonist
with a low-fit topic**. That is the hardest possible case: no real stakes, and
nothing about seaside towns that self-evidently belongs to Joan and Arthur.

Both things can be true: the note *does* rescue a low-fit topic, and the demo
was low-fit to begin with. But the remedy that follows is different. "Emphasise
the note" treats the symptom. Choosing an exemplar with fit removes the need for
a rescue.

**Test before building:** show a cold reader a themed-party favpoll with no note
and no enactment. If it lands, fit alone carries a favpoll, and the exemplar —
not the copy — was the problem all along.

## The topic catalogue is an unsold asset

136 curated topics, with items and register-appropriate placeholders. There is
**no topic index page** — only `/topics/[id]` — and nothing on the landing page
links to topics at all. The catalogue is reachable only by someone who already
knows a topic's id.

This is the organiser-side version of Gary's question. A visitor asks "what is
the poll for?"; a would-be organiser asks "what would I even poll about?" A
browsable catalogue answers the second directly, and demonstrates breadth no
feature list can claim.

# Party theme research (2026-09-23)

Caveat: no UK retailer publishes sales rankings, so this is category
**taxonomy** — what retailers stock a full range for — not demand data. A decent
proxy, not proof.

## The structural finding: the kids' market is ~60% licensed IP

Of 68 children's themes at Party Packs: Harry Potter, Bluey, Spider-Man, Barbie,
Frozen, Paw Patrol, Peppa Pig, Pokémon, Lego, Super Mario, Sonic, Encanto,
Moana, Stitch, Minions, Fortnite, Roblox, Gabby's Dollhouse, Octonauts, Hey
Duggee, Bing, Baby Shark, LOL Surprise, Taylor Swift, KPop Demon Hunters.

favpoll cannot chase that and should not want to: trademark-heavy, faddish, and
a two-year shelf life per topic. **The durable generic spine underneath it is
the part worth having.**

## Adult coverage is already strong

Of 22 adult fancy-dress categories, **seven are decades** (1920s–1990s).
`Decade` + `Music era` alone cover a third of the adult theme market. Add
`Film` / `TV programme` / `Film genre` / `Sitcom` (Film & TV), `Book` / `Author`
(Books), `Country` / `City` / `Cuisine` (Around the World) and the four
Christmas topics, and most of the adult list is covered.

`Decade` is doing a great deal of quiet work and is probably the most
under-promoted topic in the catalogue.

## The gaps

Themes on **both** lists are the strongest signal — they span ages, so they
recur across a lifetime of events rather than a single birthday.

| Theme | Kids | Adults | IP risk | In catalogue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Halloween | implicit | yes | none | no |
| Pirate | yes | yes | none | no |
| Superhero | yes | yes | **high** | no |
| Circus | yes | yes | none | no |
| Wild West | yes | yes | none | no |
| Space | yes | — | none | no |
| Farm / Jungle | yes | — | none | partial (`Animal`) |

Already covered better than expected: Unicorn/Mermaid → `Mythical creature`,
Sealife/Narwhal → `Sea creature`, Princess/Fairy → `Fairy tale`, Bug hunt →
`Insect`, Gaming → `Video game`, Football → `Football team`.

**Halloween first, by some distance.** Christmas is mined four ways and
Halloween not at all. It is the UK's second costume occasion and it is
*annual* — and annual recurrence is the property that feeds the record.

## On "splitting the record volume" — corrected (founder challenge, 2026-09-23)

An earlier draft of this note argued for restraint on new topics, on the grounds
that more topics thin the all-time rankings. **That was wrong as stated.**

Dilution only occurs when a new topic **overlaps** an existing one. Adding
`Halloween costume` does not split `Dinosaur`; those occasions were never
competing for the same pledge. The real question per topic is:

- Does it **take** picks from a topic already in the catalogue? → dilutive
- Does it **unlock** an occasion that would otherwise create no favpoll? → pure gain

At this stage the binding constraint is favpolls *created*, not topics
*available*, so the second case dominates.

Two further points cut the same way:

1. **Breadth is the record.** All-time rankings across 136 topics is a more
   interesting artefact than twenty deep ones. Narrowing the catalogue shrinks
   the product.
2. **Topics are the organiser's on-ramp.** Restricting them fights the theme-fit
   insight directly: fit is what makes a poll self-explanatory, and fit requires
   a topic that matches the occasion.

What survives is narrow and practical: **do not add a topic that competes with
one already held.** `Favourite scary film` would cannibalise `Film` and
`Film genre`; `Favourite Halloween costume` cannibalises nothing. It is a
framing question per topic, not a budget.
