## The channel: in-memory giving through funeral directors

Charitable collections at funerals are an established, sizeable and almost
entirely offline market.

- **Roughly £100 million a year** reaches charities in the UK through in-memory
  donations managed by funeral directors (industry estimate).
- Individual gifts are **£10–£30** typically, the sum a mourner would otherwise
  have spent on flowers.
- **Co-op Funeralcare conducts about 93,000 funerals a year** and has published
  its annual chart of funeral music since 2002 — which is both the scale of the
  channel and the evidence that people hold genuine favourites at a funeral.

A funeral collection today is a basket by the door or a page a family sets up
themselves. favpoll makes it the thing guests take part in: pick the song, pick
the hymn, pledge as you pick.

### Direct cremations are inside the addressable market, not outside it

**21% of UK funerals are now direct cremations** — no service, nobody present,
£1,628 against £3,828 for a simple attended funeral (SunLife Cost of Dying
2026). It is the fastest-growing fifth of the market.

A collection basket cannot serve a direct cremation: there is no room and no
door to put it by. **favpoll can, because a favpoll is a page** — a link, a
short code, a QR, pledges taken online. Nothing about it requires anyone to be
anywhere. So the fifth of the market that the existing method cannot reach at
all is inside ours, and these are precisely the families left without a way
for the people who cared to take part.

**The honest qualification is distribution, not reach.** At an attended funeral
the order of service carries the link for free, in the room, at the moment
people are most moved. A direct-cremation family passes it round themselves.
So for that segment the £400 per-favpoll figure is the uncertain number — the
"50 mourners" anchor does not apply, and the relevant figure is "people who
would have come", which we cannot yet size in either direction. We have not
adjusted the unit for it, and we would rather flag it than hide it.

## The unit

One funeral favpoll:

| | | Assumption |
|---|---|---|
| Mourners at a funeral | 50 | Typical for a UK funeral; many are larger |
| Who pledge | 20 | 40% participation |
| Average pledge | £20 | Mid-point of the £10–£30 norm |
| **Collection per favpoll** | **£400** | |

£400 is deliberately modest. It is what a single average funeral produces at
less than half participation, and it is consistent with the published
per-mourner figures rather than derived from our own hopes.

**The £20 leg is conservative by about half.** The published average in-memory
donation is **£38**, and Marie Curie alone receives around **£1 million a year**
from donations made at funeral services. We have kept £20 because it is the
mid-point of the range a mourner is reported to give, and we would rather the
figure be checkable than flattering.

## What £10,000 a year requires

**£10,000 ÷ £400 = 25 favpolls a year.** Two a month.

An independent funeral director conducting 150 funerals a year reaches that at
**17% uptake** — roughly one funeral in six offering a favpoll instead of, or
alongside, a collection basket.

| Funeral directors | Funerals/yr each | Uptake | Favpolls/yr | Donations/yr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 150 | 17% | 25 | **£10,000** |
| 3 | 150 | 10% | 45 | £18,000 |
| 10 | 150 | 15% | 225 | £90,000 |
| OurCoop, one society | 16,966 | 2% | 339 | £135,600 |
| Co-op Funeralcare alone | 93,000 | 1% | 930 | £372,000 |

**The point of the table is the first row.** One funeral director relationship,
at modest uptake, clears the threshold. We are not asking you to believe a
hockey stick; we are asking you to believe one relationship.

The two lower rows show the ceiling rather than the plan, and the more
realistic of them is OurCoop — a single independent co-operative society formed
in January 2026, trading as FuneralCoop across more than 150 funeral homes,
which conducted 16,966 funerals last year. One society, one named decision, and
a sector of five or six more like it. Co-op Funeralcare's 93,000 is there only
for the scale of the channel.

## Why funeral directors, and why they say yes

- **It solves their problem, not ours.** In-memory donations are
  administratively thankless for a funeral director: cash to count, cheques to
  bank, a family to report back to. favpoll removes the handling entirely.
- **It is the family's choice, not a sale.** The director offers it alongside
  the order of service. No pressure, no fee to them.
- **Independents first.** A single-branch independent decides on the day it is
  explained to them. Co-op is the prize, not the first call.
- **It makes the service they sell bigger, not easier to skip.** Two parts of
  favpoll exist only where there is a room: a printed **insert card** that sits
  with the order of service and carries the firm's own name — "Arranged with
  [firm]" — in every mourner's hands, and a **live screen** showing the favpoll
  in the room, with the result revealed at the close. Neither is possible
  without an attended funeral. For a director that is visible enhancement and
  a piece of their own marketing, which is a better offer than saving them the
  counting of cash.
- **Two channels, not one product looking for a home.** The attended funeral is
  served by the stationery and the screen. Direct cremations are served by the
  page alone, and belong to a different set of providers who have no attended
  service to protect. A family has already chosen between the two before
  favpoll appears, so the channels do not compete for the same funeral.
- **What we lead with to a traditional director is the attended funeral.** The
  direct-cremation point above is a statement about the size of the market, for
  you — not a pitch to a funeral director. A direct cremation's whole value to
  a family is that there is less of it, so offering a director a way to add
  something to their lowest-margin service helps them least, and risks sounding
  like either an upsell to the bereaved or a criticism of their service.

## Honest risks, since you will find them anyway

- **We have raised nothing to date.** Our pledge button is withheld until a
  charity has agreed, and no charity has been approached, which is why the
  number is zero rather than small. The route to a charity was the thing we
  would not improvise — hence this application.
- **No funeral-director relationship is signed yet.** The channel reasoning is
  sound and the economics are modest, but the first row of that table is a
  target, not a contract. We would rather say so. Two conversations are open,
  both by referral from a local celebrant: **Gavin Palin Funeral Services** in
  Nantwich, a five-person family firm, and **Crewe & Nantwich Funeral
  Service**. Neither has committed to anything, and we are asking them for
  professional judgement on whether the thing is appropriate before we ask them
  for business.
- **Seasonality and sensitivity.** A memorial is the most delicate setting a
  product can operate in. It is also why we built a consent gate before a
  pledge button, and why your discretion clause mattered enough to us that we
  worked out our own answer to it: if a chosen charity cannot be funded, we
  tell the family, ask for an alternative, and would rather match the donation
  ourselves than leave it unexplained.

## Beyond funerals

The same mechanic runs on birthdays, weddings, retirements, christenings and
sponsored challenges, and those will be the larger share in time. We lead with
funerals because the collection already exists there, which makes favpoll a
replacement for something rather than a new habit.
